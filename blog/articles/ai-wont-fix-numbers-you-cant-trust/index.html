Data foundations November 13, 2026

AI won't fix numbers you can't trust

There is a lot of pressure right now to do something with AI. So a tool gets bought, a pilot gets built, and a dashboard gets a chat box. Six months later the leadership meeting is stuck on the same two questions it was stuck on before. What should we do? And can we trust the number we're basing it on?

If that sounds familiar, you're in good company. RAND interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers about why AI projects fail, and the root causes they heard have very little to do with AI. The first one on their list is that people misunderstand, or miscommunicate, what problem needs to be solved. The others include not having the data the project needs, chasing the latest technology over a real problem, and not having the plumbing in place to manage the data at all.

We agree with that list, and we'd go one step further. A fancy dashboard or tool that doesn't drive a decision is just another shiny object. AI can do a lot for a business, but it sits on top of a foundation, and most of the disappointment comes from skipping it.

Here is the order we'd do things in.

Start with the decision, not the dashboard

A report earns its keep when someone does something different because of it. Most reports don't get that far. They describe.

Say your monthly sales update reads like this:

Sales were $2.1 million in March, down 3% from February. Returns were up. Three new accounts opened.

All of it can be true and none of it tells anyone what to do. Now the same month, written for a decision:

March was down 3%, and almost all of it came from two accounts that cut their orders. One is fixable with a call this week. The other is a pricing question, and we need an answer by the 15th.

That's an illustration with made-up numbers, but you can probably picture your own version. The data behind both is identical. The second one took someone asking "so what?" until there was an answer.

Before you build or buy anything, write down the decision it's for and who makes it. If you can't, you're not ready to build it, and no tool will fix that for you.

Make sure the number is right before you make it fast

This is the part people skip, because a wrong number rarely looks wrong.

To show how easily it happens, we took a large public file and checked it the way you'd want your own checked. Iowa publishes every wholesale liquor order its licensed retailers place. The 2025 file has about three million rows. Add up the dollars and you get $515.6 million.

The real figure in that file is $424.8 million. One section of it is included twice, so late summer is counted double and the year comes out 21% too high. Nothing about the file looks broken. It opens fine, the columns are tidy, and every chart you could build from it would show a healthy bump in August and September.

We caught it with a count of rows per month, which takes a few minutes. A dashboard pointed at that file would have drawn the bump. An AI assistant asked "what was our best month?" would have answered September, confidently, and been wrong.

Speed is the easy part to buy. Being right has to come first.

Agree on what the words mean

Ask a simple question of the same file: who is the biggest customer?

Go by store and the biggest one is 3.2% of the year. That looks like a nicely spread customer base. Group the stores that carry one grocery chain's name and that chain is 30.3%. Both answers are correct. They answer different questions, and only one of them matters when a contract is up for renewal.

The file also has 95 stores that appear under two different names. Most are convenience stores that changed brands when the Kum & Go name was retired. Count customers by name and every one of them shows up as a customer you lost and a new one you won. It was the same building ordering the same products.

Every company has words like this. Customer. Active. Revenue. Margin. If sales, finance and operations each mean something slightly different, they'll each bring a different number to the meeting, and the meeting will be about the numbers instead of the decision.

Then bring in AI

None of this is an argument against AI. Once the three things above are in place, it's useful. It can answer routine questions in seconds, for people who would never open a report. It can watch for the month that's out of line so nobody has to remember to check.

But it inherits whatever it's given. It will read a file that double-counts August and tell you August was great. It doesn't know your company has two definitions of a customer unless someone has written one down. The foundation is what turns AI from a demo into something you can run the business on.

Measure what changed

"We launched the dashboard" isn't a result. Neither is "everyone has a license."

Pick the outcome before you start. A decision that used to take two weeks and now takes two days. A monthly close with one less manual step. An error that stopped reaching customers. If you can't name what should change, that's worth knowing before the invoice arrives.

Where to start this week

You don't need a new tool for any of this. Take the one report your leadership team uses most and do three things.

  1. Write the decision it supports at the top. If there isn't one, ask whether anyone would miss the report.
  2. Count the rows per month in the data underneath it. A month that's far out of line is a question to answer before it's a trend.
  3. Pick one word on it, like "customer" or "revenue", and ask three people what it means. Write down the answer you agree on.

It's unglamorous work, and it decides whether everything you build afterward can be trusted. This is what Angler BI does: we find what's wrong or misleading in the numbers, get to the real problem underneath, and help you make the decisions that follow.
